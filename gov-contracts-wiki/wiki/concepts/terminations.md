# Terminations (Convenience & Default)

**One-line:** The Government can end a contract at will (T4C, paying
costs + settlement but no lost profits) or for contractor failure (T4D,
paying only for value received and charging excess reprocurement costs)
— and most termination fights are about which one it should have been.

*Compiled from 2026 Acquisition Attorneys Deskbook chs. 20 & 21 (whole
chapters).*

## Termination for Convenience (RFO FAR Part 49; ch. 20)

### The right and its limits
- Broad right to terminate in whole or part whenever in the
  Government's interest; no hearing required, no duty to terminate to
  help a losing contractor. The T4C clause is read into contracts by
  operation of law under the **Christian doctrine** (*G.L. Christian*,
  312 F.2d 418 (Ct. Cl. 1963)) as a deeply-ingrained procurement
  policy.
- **Standard of review (Krygoski/Kalvar line):** T4C stands unless the
  contractor proves **bad faith or abuse of discretion** (*Krygoski*, 94
  F.3d 1537 (Fed. Cir. 1996)); good faith is presumed and rebuttable
  only by clear and convincing evidence (*Am-Pro*, 281 F.3d 1234).
  *Torncello*'s "changed circumstances" test is dead letter but still
  gets argued. Terminating just to re-buy cheaper elsewhere may be bad
  faith; knowing at award you might terminate later is fine (*McHugh v.
  DLT Solutions*, 618 F.3d 1375).
- Bad-faith T4C = breach → contractor can get common-law damages
  including lost profits (*Apex Int'l*, ASBCA 38087; *Sigal Constr.*,
  CBCA 508 — deleting work to give it to a cheaper contractor).
- **Constructive T4C**: a wrongful stop-work/"cancellation" gets
  retroactively justified as a convenience termination (*G.C.
  Casebolt*, 421 F.2d 710) — but can't be used retroactively to dodge an
  unordered ID/IQ minimum (*Maxima*, 847 F.2d 1549; *Ace-Federal*, 226
  F.3d 1329).
- Erroneous T4Ds convert to T4Cs; a T4C cannot later become a T4D.
  Reinstatement of a terminated contract requires the contractor's
  written consent.

### Mechanics
- Notice per RFO FAR 49.102 (certified mail/hand/email with receipt);
  DoD congressional notification if ≥100 contractor jobs cut (DFARS
  249.7001). TCO negotiates the settlement.
- **Deductive change vs. partial T4C**: minor, segregable deletions =
  deductive change under the Changes clause; major deletions = partial
  T4C. Different recovery, different burdens — see
  [Contract Changes](contract-changes.md).

### Settlement
- Contractor gets: price for accepted work + costs incurred on
  terminated work + fair profit on work done (NOT on settlement
  expenses, and no profit if the contract was in a **loss position** —
  loss ratio applied instead, Government's burden to prove) +
  settlement expenses. **Never anticipatory profits or consequential
  damages.** Ceiling = contract price (+ settlement expenses + valid
  pending claims).
- Settlement proposal due within **1 year** of termination notice or
  the CO may unilaterally determine the amount. Inventory basis
  preferred; total-cost basis is a disfavored last resort.
- T4C effectively converts the terminated portion of an FFP contract to
  cost-reimbursement: FAR 31.205-42 termination cost principles,
  applied "subject to" the FAR 49.201 fairness principle (business
  judgment over strict accounting, *Codex*, 226 Ct. Cl. 693).
- Common items usable on other work: not allowable absent proof of
  loss. Subcontract settlements must be arm's-length and vetted by the
  prime.
- Impasse over the settlement converts the proposal into a **CDA
  claim** (*James M. Ellet*, 93 F.3d 1537).

### Commercial products/services (FAR 12.304 + 52.212-4(l))
Part 49 does NOT apply. Recovery = percentage of contract price for
work performed (including work in process, *TriRAD*, ASBCA 58855) +
reasonable charges resulting from the termination.

### Fiscal rules (ch. 20 § VI)
- Termination doesn't immediately deobligate everything — CO holds back
  estimated settlement costs.
- Expired funds generally die with a T4C and can't fund a replacement,
  **except** (1) court/GAO-ordered terminations or (2) CO-documented
  clearly-erroneous awards — and then only if: good-faith original
  award, continuing bona fide need, same size/scope replacement, no
  undue delay (DoD: ≤100 days is safe), and written findings if the CO
  made the call.

## Termination for Default (RFO FAR 49.4; ch. 21)

### Stakes and posture
- A "contractual death sentence": past-performance poison even while on
  appeal (*Colonial Press*, B-403632). CO's T4D is a final decision —
  1-year clock to COFC; review there is **de novo**, and the
  **Government bears the burden** of proving the default proper
  (*Lisbon Contractors*, 828 F.2d 759). Then the contractor must prove
  excuses.

### Grounds
1. **Failure to deliver/perform on time** — time is of the essence;
   strict compliance with specs is owed; substantial
   compliance/completion doctrine gives minor-shortfall contractors a
   chance to cure.
2. **Failure to make progress endangering performance** — CO needs a
   reasoned basis that timely completion is not reasonably likely
   (percent complete vs. time left, milestones, subs, finances, track
   record; *McDonnell Douglas*, 323 F.3d 1006).
3. **Failure to perform any other provision** — must be a **material**
   requirement (insurance, licensed drivers, QC plan, Davis-Bacon
   records). Not a ground in construction clauses, but courts reach the
   same result through the diligence prong.
4. **Common law**: anticipatory repudiation (express, unequivocal —
   abandonment counts) and failure to give **adequate assurances** after
   a demand (*Danzig v. AEC*, 224 F.3d 1333).
5. **Grounds unknown at termination still count** — the Government can
   defend a T4D on any valid basis that existed, even if the CO didn't
   know it (*Kirk Bros.*, 16 F.3d 1173; *Joseph Morton* — fraud).

### Notices (the trap zone)
| Situation | Cure notice required? |
|---|---|
| Late delivery (date passed) | No |
| Failure to make progress | **Yes** (10-day minimum) — except construction |
| Failure of other provision | **Yes** — except construction |
| Anticipatory repudiation | No |
| Construction (52.249-10) | No (but often given anyway) |
| Fraud / false certification | No (incurable) |
- Cure notice must specify the failures concretely and allow ≥10 days.
  Show-cause notices are never required but "courts like to see them,"
  they document defenses, and they **defeat waiver**.
- T4D notice contents per RFO FAR 49.402-3(j) (acts constituting
  default, excess-cost warning, appeal rights). Report to FAPIIS.
  Surety and SBA (small business) copies required.

### Contractor defenses
- **Excusable delay**: beyond the control and without fault/negligence
  of contractor *and* sub (acts of God, sovereign/contractual acts of
  the Government, fires, floods, epidemics, strikes, unusually severe
  weather). Sub problems excuse only if the cause was beyond both
  parties' control **and** the item wasn't available elsewhere in time
  (*General Injectables*, 519 F.3d 1360). Construction: 10-day written
  notice requirement.
- **Waiver (DeVito doctrine)**: Government forbearance past the due
  date + contractor's detrimental reliance on continued performance =
  waived delivery date (*DeVito*, 413 F.2d 1147). Largely inapplicable
  to construction (payment + LD clauses negate reliance). Acceptance of
  one late installment doesn't waive future ones. Fix: **reestablish a
  reasonable, specific schedule** (bilateral = presumed reasonable;
  unilateral must be objectively reasonable); a cure notice alone
  doesn't do it.
- Others: Government prior material breach; financial incapacity only
  if Government-caused; bankruptcy stays termination; commercial
  impracticability (very high bar — 105% cost growth insufficient).

### Decision discipline (where terminations get reversed)
- Discretionary, never compelled; must reflect the CO's **independent
  judgment** (*Schlesinger*, 390 F.2d 702 — congressional pressure;
  *TigerSwan*, 118 Fed. Cl. 447 — deferring to program office).
- Consider the RFO FAR 49.402-3(i) factors and document in a memo;
  skipping them isn't automatically fatal but feeds abuse-of-discretion
  arguments (*DCX v. Perry*, 79 F.3d 132). Consider alternatives
  (revised schedule, sub completion, no-cost cancel) per 49.402-4.
  Terminating on materially wrong facts = abuse of discretion.

### Consequences
- **Government pays only for value received**: supply — accepted items
  only (work-in-progress is the contractor's loss); services/
  construction — reasonable value of work done; cost-type — allowable
  costs + fee on accepted work.
- **Contractor liability**: excess reprocurement costs (Government must
  show similarity, actual excess cost, and reasonable mitigation) +
  liquidated damages (not for excusable-delay periods; unenforceable if
  punitive) + common-law damages. Unliquidated progress payments come
  back.
- **Fulford doctrine**: contractor may attack the underlying T4D in a
  timely appeal of the excess-cost assessment even after missing the
  T4D appeal window (post-*Maropakis* durability debated — safer to
  appeal the T4D itself).
- Erroneous T4D **converts to T4C** by clause; bad faith unlocks breach
  damages instead.
- Fiscal: original funds remain available to reprocure the unfinished
  work (replacement contract must be timely, same need, same
  size/scope; DoD expired-fund reprocurements >$4M need USD(C)).

### Commercial: "termination for cause" (52.212-4(m))
Part 49 doesn't apply. Grounds include any default or failure to give
adequate assurances; improper cause terminations convert to convenience
terminations; FAPIIS reporting required.

## Gotchas / lessons learned
- Government side: match the notice to the ground (the cure-notice
  table above), reestablish schedules after forbearance, and write the
  49.402-3(i) memo. Most reversed T4Ds die on process, not substance.
- Contractor side: calendar the 1-year settlement-proposal deadline and
  the 1-year T4D appeal; respond substantively to every cure notice —
  silence reads as inadequate assurance.
- Both: "no lost profits" is the iron rule of T4C recovery; the only
  way around it is proving bad faith, which almost never succeeds.
